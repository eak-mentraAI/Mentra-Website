AI tutors that don't just hand over the answer are no longer rare. Several free study modes now coach a student step by step, and most students can open one in seconds.

That's good news for a curious student working on their own. A district has a different job. It needs to know that students are learning, that teachers can act on what they see, and that children's data is handled properly.

So the useful question isn't "does it give answers?" It's "who is it built for?" These five questions help you tell the difference. They work for any vendor, including us. Under each one, we've said how Mentra answers it.

## 1. Who controls the help: the student or the teacher?

Many study modes let the student decide how much help to get, including switching back to direct answers. That's reasonable for an adult learner. In a classroom, the teacher usually needs to decide.

**Ask the vendor:** Can a teacher set the level of AI help for a specific assignment? Can they switch it off completely for a check of unaided work? Can a student override that?

**How Mentra answers:** Teachers set the AI help for each assignment: No AI Help, Hints Only, Guided Support or Full AI Assistance, with a limit on hints per question. Sprig's teaching moves are chosen by fixed rules, not by the AI model, and each reply is checked before the student sees it, so answers aren't handed over.

## 2. Can teachers see which progress was the student's own?

A correct answer reached with three hints looks the same on a worksheet as one reached alone. If the tool can't tell those apart, teachers lose the signal they need most.

**Ask the vendor:** Does the teacher see how each answer was reached? What exactly counts as "independent", and can the teacher check the evidence behind a label?

**How Mentra answers:** The Independence Report labels each answer *on their own*, *with help*, or *repeated what Sprig said*. An answer only counts as unaided if it was a real attempt, no help was active, and it doesn't echo Sprig's wording. Every label links to the turn behind it, so a teacher can check it.

## 3. Does it admit when it doesn't know yet?

Dashboards that always show a number feel reassuring, but a confident read on thin evidence can send a teacher in the wrong direction.

**Ask the vendor:** What does the report show for a student with very little work so far? How much evidence does it need before it draws a conclusion?

**How Mentra answers:** The report makes no call until a student has at least five unaided attempts across three sessions and two concepts within 14 days. Before that, it says "not enough data yet". Recommendations show their confidence and say when they may be wrong.

## 4. Who decides what happens next?

AI can suggest a next step. It shouldn't take one on its own, especially for a child.

**Ask the vendor:** When the tool recommends something, what happens if the teacher does nothing? Can the teacher change or dismiss it? Is there a record of what changed afterwards?

**How Mentra answers:** Recommendations wait for the teacher to approve, modify or dismiss them. Approving one doesn't trigger anything automatically, and the teacher can track the outcome afterwards. Families get one thing to try at home, through the school.

## 5. What does it actually do with children's data?

"Compliant" on a slide isn't enough. Ask what the system does.

**Ask the vendor:** Is consent required for every student? What happens when a student's age is unknown? Can parents withdraw consent, request deletion, or see what was said? How is one district's data kept apart from another's? What happens in a safety concern?

**How Mentra answers:** Every student needs active consent, recorded by a parent or the school, and an unknown age is treated as under 13. Parents can withdraw consent, request deletion, and export the last 30 days of their child's AI conversations, redacted for privacy. Each district's data is separated at the database level. Every message gets a safety check before any teaching, filters are tuned to each age band, and teachers receive safety alerts. A backup AI provider stays switched off until it's approved for student data.

## Then test it in a pilot you can actually judge

The strongest evidence is your own. Before you start a pilot, agree how you'll know whether it worked:

- **Measure unaided work outside the tool.** Use tasks students do without AI, before and after the pilot.
- **Compare with similar classes.** A comparison group tells you whether a gain came from the tool.
- **Agree the success criteria up front.** Decide what counts as a good result before anyone sees the data.
- **Track teacher time and use.** Note how often teachers act on what they see, and how long it takes.

## A quick checklist

Take this into any vendor conversation:

1. Can the teacher set, and switch off, AI help for each assignment?
2. Does the report separate answers reached with help from unaided ones, with evidence a teacher can check?
3. Does it say "not enough data yet" instead of guessing?
4. Do recommendations wait for a teacher's decision?
5. Is consent required for every student, and is each district's data kept separate?
6. Will the vendor agree to a pilot judged on unaided work against a comparison group?
